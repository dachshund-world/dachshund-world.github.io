Dachshund Winter Coat Fit Guide: How to Measure a Long Back, Choose Harness-Over vs Harness-Under, and Layer by Coat Type

A coat that fits a terrier will not fit a dachshund. Measure the long back, the chest and the neck, pick the right cut, and decide whether the harness belongs over the coat or under it.

Quick answer

Measure three things before you buy: back length from the base of the neck to the base of the tail, chest girth at the widest point just behind the elbows, and neck where the collar sits. Buy for the chest, check the back length, then decide whether the harness goes over or under the coat. Long-backed dachshunds usually need a dachshund-specific cut rather than a generic small-dog size.

A dachshund winter coat fit guide starts with a tape measure, not a size label. A coat cut for a terrier will sit short on a long back, while one chosen only by chest girth can hang past the tail, bunch at the shoulders, or leave the rear half of the spine uncovered. This guide walks through the three measurements that matter, the harness-over versus harness-under decision, and the small layering changes that suit smooth, longhaired and wirehaired dachshunds.

What are the three measurements a dachshund winter coat needs?

Back length, chest girth and neck. Everything else — the size band, the harness routing, whether you need a long-back cut — follows from those three numbers.

The estimator below turns your three measurements into a starting size band and a harness recommendation. It is an editorial estimate for dachshunds, not a brand size chart, so always compare the result with the specific coat maker's own table before ordering.

Dachshund winter coat size estimator

Measure in centimetres with a soft tape while your dachshund stands square on all four feet. Nothing is saved or sent.

The three measurements are the same ones Pet Circle’s vet guide demonstrates on camera: from the base of the neck to the base of the tail for length, right around the widest part of the chest just behind the elbows, and around the neck where a collar would sit with room for two fingers underneath. The clip below shows the tape placement on a real dog, which is easier to copy than a diagram.

One rule from that video is worth repeating: if your dachshund lands between two sizes, go up rather than down. A coat that is slightly long can be adjusted at the chest and neck; a coat that is too tight cannot be fixed without restricting the shoulders.

How do you measure a long back without guessing?

Start the tape where the collar sits, follow the spine flat to the base of the tail, and stop where the tail begins — not at the tip. Measure while your dachshund is standing, not curled on the sofa.

Back length: base of neck to base of tail

This is the measurement most generic dog-coat charts get wrong. The FCI breed standard describes the dachshund’s body length as the distance from the point of the manubrium of the sternum to the point of the buttock, and expects it to be about 1.7 to 1.8 times the height at the withers — a formal way of saying the back is long by design. A coat cut for a terrier back will leave the rear half of a dachshund exposed, so look for a long-back or breed-specific cut when your dog's back measures much more than its chest is deep.

Use the back measurement as a length filter, not a size filter. The size band still comes from chest girth, because that is where a coat binds or billows. Keep the tape flat: a tape that dips into the waist will read short, and one that arches over the shoulders will read long.

Chest girth: the widest point behind the elbows

Wrap the tape around the deepest part of the ribcage, just behind the front legs. Breathe normally, keep the tape snug but not tight, and write down the number to the nearest half centimetre. The FCI standard measures chest circumference from the highest point of the withers to the lowest point of the chest with a lightly tensioned tape, and uses it as an official size divider: standard dachshund males measure over 37 cm up to 47 cm, standard females over 35 cm up to 45 cm, with miniature and rabbit sizes below that.

Because chest circumference already defines dachshund size classes, it is the most reliable single number for a coat. Wikipedia notes that some kennel clubs, including Germany's, measure chest circumference in addition to height and weight — further evidence that the chest, not the weight on the bag, is the number to trust.

Neck: where the collar sits, with two fingers of room

Measure the neck at the height where a collar normally rests, not at the widest part of the head. The tape should be snug enough to stay put and loose enough that two fingers slide underneath comfortably. A coat neck that is too wide lets cold air and rain down the back; one that is too narrow rubs the throat and can make your dachshund refuse to walk.

Harness-over or harness-under: which is right for your dachshund?

Harness-under means the harness sits against the dog and the coat goes on top; harness-over means the coat goes on first and the harness is buckled over it. Neither is universally better — the right choice depends on chest depth, coat length, and whether the coat has a proper harness opening.

When harness-under wins

A deep-chested dachshund benefits from keeping the harness close to the body, where it was fitted. Putting a padded coat between the dog and the harness can change the strap tension, so if your harness was fitted on the snug side, harness-under is the cleaner choice — provided the coat has a real harness slot rather than a slit that stretches out of shape. Check that the slot sits over the D-ring without twisting the coat.

When harness-over wins

On a very long back, a coat with no slot tends to migrate rearward as your dachshund trots. A harness buckled over the coat acts like a belt: it holds the coat in place, keeps the leash attachment visible, and lets you tighten or loosen the fit without taking the coat off. This is also the practical option for wet winter walks when you expect to take the coat off at the door and leave the harness on.

What about a step-in harness?

Step-in harnesses are popular with dachshund owners because they are easy to put on a low-built dog. If you use one under a coat, check that the coat's underside does not cover the point where the two side panels meet; if you use one over a coat, make sure the coat is not so thick that the step-in panels cannot sit flat. Our guide to what to check before buying a step-in harness covers the fit points that matter for this breed.

How should you layer by coat type: smooth, longhaired, wirehaired?

The FCI recognises three dachshund coat varieties — smooth-haired, long-haired and wire-haired — and each changes how a winter coat should sit. The goal is the same for all three: one insulating layer close to the body, a weather layer on top, and no strap or seam pressing into the dog.

Smooth-haired dachshunds

The smooth coat is short, dense and close-fitting, so there is little natural loft between the skin and the air. That makes a smooth dachshund the most straightforward to fit: a single fleece-lined coat usually sits cleanly, and you rarely need to size up for hair. Check the chest and armpit area carefully, because a short coat shows rub marks quickly. A smooth coat also makes harness-under easier, since there is no feathering to trap under a strap.

Longhaired dachshunds

Longhaired dachshunds carry silky feathering on the legs, ears and underside of the tail, and the FCI standard describes the coat as longer at the throat and under the body. That hair adds bulk exactly where a coat's chest panel and belly strap sit. Choose a coat with a roomier chest or an adjustable belly strap, and check that the feathering is not folded back on itself under the harness. Dogs Trust notes that longhaired dachshunds need more regular brushing than smooth or wirehaired ones, so keep the coat's lining clean and the hair untangled where the straps cross. Our longhaired dachshund trimming guide covers paws, ears and tail.

Wirehaired dachshunds

The wirehaired coat has a dense, wiry topcoat with an undercoat, plus a beard and bushy eyebrows. That dense topcoat and undercoat do a lot of the insulating work, but the texture can catch on fleece linings and Velcro. Look for a smooth lining, avoid tight chest straps that flatten the wiry coat, and do not assume the coat needs to be as warm as a smooth dachshund's. If you strip the coat by hand, plan the fit check after grooming rather than before, because removing dead coat changes how a harness sits. The wirehaired hand-stripping guide explains the timing.

Step-by-step: the quick fit check

Do this at home, on a dry day, before the first cold walk. You need a tape measure, the coat, and the harness you actually use.

  1. Measure once, write it down. Record back, chest and neck in centimetres. Re-measure the chest if your dachshund has been clipped or has gained or lost weight.
  2. Put the coat on over a light indoor walk. Watch the shoulders: the front edge should sit behind the shoulder blade, not on top of it.
  3. Check the tail end. The coat should finish at the base of the tail. If it hangs over the tail, it will collect snow and urine; if it stops mid-back, the rear spine is uncovered.
  4. Slide two fingers under the chest closure. You should be able to do this without forcing. If not, size up or loosen the belly strap.
  5. Add the harness in your chosen configuration. Clip the lead on and walk your dachshund up and down the hallway. The coat should not rotate, bunch, or lift at the rear.
  6. Test a sit and a lie-down. A coat that is fine standing can pull across the back when your dog sits. Watch the neck opening and the rear hem.
  7. Check the leg openings. No seam should press into the armpit or the front of the thigh. Red marks after a short indoor walk mean the cut is wrong, not that the dog needs to get used to it.
  8. Do a short outdoor trial. A few minutes in the garden is enough to show whether the coat stays put at a trot. Keep the tags on until you are sure.

Common winter coat fit mistakes

  • Buying by weight or by "small". Dachshund chests vary enormously within the same weight. Use the chest girth, then check the back length.
  • Choosing a coat that is too short. A coat that ends mid-back looks neat but leaves the long lumbar area exposed to wind and wet.
  • Sizing down for a slimmer look. If your dachshund is between sizes, the safer choice is up, with the belly strap adjusted shorter.
  • Ignoring the harness slot alignment. A slot that sits well off the D-ring twists the coat with every step and rubs the chest.
  • Layering a puffy coat under a tight harness. The harness no longer fits the dog it was adjusted for. Decide harness-over or harness-under, then fit the harness in that configuration.
  • Forgetting the coat type. A longhaired dachshund needs more room at the chest and throat; a wirehaired one needs a smoother lining; a smooth one shows rub marks first.
  • Never re-checking the fit. A coat that fitted in November can be tight by January if your dachshund has put on winter weight. Re-measure at the chest.

Dachshund winter coat FAQ

How do I measure my dachshund for a winter coat?
Measure the back from the base of the neck to the base of the tail, the chest at its widest point just behind the elbows, and the neck where a collar sits with room for two fingers. Use the chest girth for the size band and the back length to choose the cut. Keep the tape flat and measure while your dachshund stands square.
What if my dachshund is between two coat sizes?
Go up rather than down. A slightly longer coat can be adjusted at the chest and belly, while a tight coat cannot be made roomier without restricting the shoulders. Then check that the longer back does not hang over the tail.
Should the harness go over or under a dachshund coat?
Use harness-under when your dachshund is deep-chested and the coat has a reinforced harness slot that lines up with the D-ring. Use harness-over when the back is very long, when the coat has no slot, or on wet walks where you want the harness to hold the coat in place. Fit the harness in the configuration you will actually use.
Do longhaired dachshunds need a different coat fit?
Yes. Feathering on the chest, legs and tail adds bulk under the chest panel and belly strap, so choose a coat with more chest room or an adjustable belly strap. Keep the hair untangled where the straps cross and check for folds under the harness.
How do I know a coat is too tight around the chest?
You cannot slide two fingers under the closed chest closure, the coat leaves red marks behind the front legs, or your dachshund stands still and refuses to walk. A coat that bunches behind the elbows is also too tight, even if the chest measurement matched the label.
How long should a dachshund coat be?
It should reach the base of the tail without covering the tail itself. A coat that stops mid-back leaves the long lumbar area exposed, while one that hangs past the tail collects snow and gets in the way when your dog toilets.
Can my dachshund wear a coat with a step-in harness?
Yes, provided the coat has a slot or opening that lets the step-in panels sit flat and the lead clip reach the D-ring without twisting. If the coat covers the point where the panels meet, wear the harness over the coat instead.

The bottom line

A dachshund winter coat only works if it is measured, not guessed. Take the three numbers, buy for the chest, confirm the back length reaches the tail base, and choose harness-over or harness-under based on chest depth and coat construction. Then match the cut to the coat type in front of you: smooth coats fit closely, longhaired coats need chest room, and wirehaired coats need a smooth lining. Do that once and the same coat will see your dachshund through the whole season.
