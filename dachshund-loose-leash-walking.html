Dachshund Pulling on the Leash: A Loose-Leash Walking Plan for Long, Low Dogs

Your dachshund's nose has a vote in every walk — but it doesn't get to do the steering. Here's a calm, phase-by-phase plan to change what a tight leash means, without a prong collar or a battle.

Stop-and-plantFront-door calmSniff-firstNovember 9, 2026
Quick answer

Teach a pulling dachshund by rewiring what a tight leash predicts: fit a well-fitted harness, stop dead the moment the leash goes tight, wait for slack or a glance back, then reward and move on. Start in a boring room, move to the front door, then graduate to quiet streets — using high-value treats and generous sniffing as the payoff for walking loose.

If your dachshund turns every walk into a sled race, you're not alone — and you're also not dealing with a "naughty" dog. Dachshunds were bred to trail and dig, and their default answer to almost everything is forward, at full speed, nose first. This plan works with that instinct instead of fighting it, and it's built around three things that matter for a long, low dog: a harness that protects the neck, short reward-based sessions, and a nose that's allowed to work.

Why dachshunds pull so hard

Pulling isn't stubbornness — it's a scent hound doing what scent hounds do. The Dachshund Club of America's breed standard describes the breed as "clever, lively and courageous to the point of rashness, persevering in above- and below-ground work," and notes that the dachshund's keen nose gives him an advantage over most other breeds for trailing. When your dog's nose locks onto something, walking forward isn't a choice he makes — it's the only option the instinct offers.

The second reason pulling persists is simpler: it works. Every step the leash dragging you forward produces is a reward for the pull, repeated dozens of times per walk. The goal of training is to make the loop run in reverse — walking with slack pays, walking into tension doesn't. If you'd like to see the full method on a real dachshund before you start, this clip from The Cagdot covers switching the harness, the stop-and-plant technique, high-value treats, boring practice spots and intentional sniff breaks — the same building blocks in the same order as this guide.

Gear first: harness, leash and the right reward

Before a single training step, set up your equipment. A well-fitted harness — not a collar — is the right starting point for a long-backed dog, and the step-in harness guide on this site walks through the fit checks that matter, from strap positions to how much room your dachshund's shoulders actually need.

Choose a leash you can handle comfortably — 4–6 feet (about 1.2–1.8 m) is enough to give slack without giving a free-for-all. Retractable leashes have no place in this plan: they teach your dog that tension has no consequences, which is the exact opposite of what we're building.

Make the reward worth leaving a squirrel for

Reward-based training only works if the reward outranks the environment. The RSPCA's training guidance sums it up: give your dog something they really like when they show a behaviour, and they're more likely to do it again — and the RSPCA recommends always starting lessons in a quiet room, keeping sessions short, and ending on a positive note.

Test your treats at home first. Boiled chicken, cheese or a hot-dog sliver are typically in a different league than kibble. If your dachshund ignores even premium treats outside, that's your first signal: the environment is too exciting, and you need to step back in difficulty, not up in bribes.

Phase 1 — Calm the front door

Most dachshunds don't start pulling at the park — they start two steps before the front door, because the door has become the starting gun. Fix that first, in a boring room with the leash already on.

  1. Put the leash on and drop it. Let your dachshund wear the harness and leash around the house in short sessions until they're a non-event, not a cue to run.
  2. Reward calm. Every time your dog sits, lies down, or simply stands with a loose leash, quietly mark it and drop a treat. You're teaching: nothing needed, leash loose, food appears.
  3. Move to the door. Approach the front door. If he pushes ahead of you, stop, step back one pace, and wait for slack before you take a single step toward the door again.
  4. Open the door — without leaving. Open it, close it. Repeat until opening the door no longer triggers a bolt. Then open it and step onto the mat, close it, come back in. The magic rule is: the walk only continues when the leash is loose.

Tip: keep these sessions to 3–5 minutes. The RSPCA's advice to keep training short applies double here — a dachshund's patience runs out fast when there's a door involved.

Phase 2 — Stop-and-plant on quiet streets

This is the core mechanic. Everything else in this plan supports it: the moment the leash tightens, movement stops, and it only restarts when there's slack.

  1. Pick a boring route. An empty sidewalk, a quiet cul-de-sac — not the dog park. You want as few trigger smells as possible for the first sessions.
  2. Walk at your pace, and plant. The instant the leash goes tight, stop moving — no yank, no pulling back, just a tree trunk in a harness. Most people make the mistake of correcting with a pull; instead, do absolutely nothing but stop.
  3. Wait for slack or an offer. Watch for the leash to soften, or for your dachshund to turn his head back toward you. The moment it happens, mark with a "yes," drop a treat at your feet, and walk off in a fresh direction.
  4. Change direction often. If he lurches toward a scent, reverse. Now he's following you to keep the walk going, instead of you being towed. This does the quiet, patient work of teaching that tension ends forward motion but never causes it.
  5. End on a win. Every session should finish with a minute of easy loose-leash walking or a relaxed sniff, then a treat scatter and head home. You're storing "loose leash = good things" in muscle memory.

For a long, low back: never correct a dachshund by jerking or lifting the leash — and never walk a pulling dog on a collar. Sharp pressure on the neck is exactly what this breed's spine doesn't need. Pulling is solved by changing the consequence, not by winning a tug of war. Pair the harness with calm transitions like a trained ramp routine so the whole day protects that back.

Phase 3 — Add duration, then real-world walks

Once stop-and-plant works on quiet streets, start raising the stakes a few minutes at a time.

  1. Extend by one minute. Add a minute to the loose-leash portion every few days. Rushing to a 40-minute walk too early will just undo a week of work.
  2. Add one distraction at a time. A person on the same side of the street, then a person with a dog at a distance, then a busier park corner. If your dachshund goes over threshold, that's distance for the next session — you got too close too fast.
  3. Randomize rewards. Start paying intermittently. Loose walking becomes the norm when the dog never knows which loose step pays, which is exactly what keeps him guessing instead of straining.
  4. Let the nose finish the job. End every real walk with permission to sniff — a reward your dachshund understands better than any treat. And remember a dachshund's real energy needs are more moderate than their enthusiasm suggests; a structured short walk that is loose on the leash beats a long dragging one every time.

Sniffing is part of the plan, not the enemy

Loose-leash walking and heel work are different skills, and the distinction matters for a scent hound. As dog owners on r/puppy101 put it in a popular explainer, "there is a difference between loose leash walking and a heel" — a heel means the dog stays glued to your side, while a loose leash means the leash simply never goes tight. Your dachshund can walk ahead, drift to the side, and investigate the world, so long as the line stays slack and you're in control.

Treat sniffing as an earned reward and you'll get further than with food alone. Make it formal: watch for a loose leash and a check-in, then say "go sniff" and give him thirty seconds of nose time. Nosework games are also excellent mental exercise — the dachshund toys and enrichment guide has ideas that tire a busy nose as effectively as a long walk.

Common mistakes that keep dachshunds pulling

  • Yanking or jerking the leash. It reads as a game or an attack, and it's rough on a long spinal column. Stop-and-plant needs no force at all.
  • Allowing tension "just this once." One slack-walk exception after three good days resets the habit. Rules have to be identical on every single walk.
  • Going straight to a busy area. Calm comes from the boring places first. The park is a reward for progress, not a training ground.
  • Forgetting the harness. On a collar, a strong puller can hurt himself before you ever get to teach him anything. Gear protects the dog while training changes the behavior.
  • Skipping the settlement at the door. Every phase starts at the door. Skip it and your dog learns the front gate is the exciting part again.
  • Making sessions too long. A tired, frustrated 25 minutes undoes what a fresh, happy 8 minutes builds. Short and consistent wins this race.

Loose-leash walking FAQ

Why does my dachshund pull so hard on the leash?
Dachshunds are scent hounds bred to trail and dig, so their default is to follow the nose forward, even when it drags you along. Pulling also works: the more a dog pulls, the more the walk progresses. It becomes a habit that has to be gently replaced.
Is a harness or collar better for a leash-pulling dachshund?
A well-fitted harness is the safer, more comfortable choice for a long-backed breed. It spreads pressure away from the neck and gives you a front clip option that turns pulling to the side. See our guide on choosing a step-in harness for what to check before you buy.
Should I let my dachshund sniff on walks?
Yes. Sniffing is a breed-defining need and it makes loose-leash walking easier, because a dog who is allowed to sniff doesn't have to race toward everything. Use sniffing as a reward: the leash stays loose while he explores, and you both move forward.
How long does it take to teach a dachshund loose-leash walking?
Expect clear progress in two to four weeks of short daily sessions, but the exact time depends on how long pulling has been rewarded and how exciting your streets are. The key is not the calendar but consistency: every single walk has to follow the same rules.
What if my dachshund won't take treats outside?
Move training back to a quieter spot, walk away from distractions first, or raise the value of the reward to something he can't resist, like boiled chicken or cheese. If he's over threshold, no food will beat the environment — reduce the excitement, not the treat.
Is loose-leash walking the same as heel?
No. A heel means the dog stays at your side in position. Loose-leash walking simply means the leash never goes tight — your dachshund can walk ahead, behind, sniff, and explore, as long as the line stays loose and you stay in control.

Give your dachshund the walk he deserves

Your dachshund isn't pulling to be difficult — he's pulling because he's a dachshund, and nobody ever taught him a better way. This plan doesn't demand perfect heel work or hours of drilling. It asks for a harness, small daily sessions, a boring route at first, and a quiet promise: tension stops the walk, slack continues it. Do that on every walk, keep the rest of his routine steady around it, and in a few weeks you'll both be at the end of a loose leash — him sniffing what he wants, and you not being towed to it.
