What Size Crate Does a Dachshund Need? Measure Nose-to-Tail for Miniature, Standard and Growing Puppies

Verdict first: a dachshund crate should be only 2–4 inches longer and taller than your dog. Measure, don’t guess from the breed label, and use a divider while a puppy grows.

Quick answer

Stand your dachshund on all four paws and measure from the tip of the nose to the base of the tail (not the tail itself). Add 2–4 inches for crate length. Measure floor to the top of the head or ears, whichever is taller, and add 2–4 inches for height. A puppy needs the adult measurements plus a divider panel, so the usable space stays small until housetraining is solid.

What size crate does a dachshund need? That depends on the dog in front of you, because a dachshund is built long and low, and “miniature”, “standard” and “rabbit” are sizes defined by chest circumference rather than by a crate label. This guide is an editorial review of the sizing method, not a product test: we did not buy or test any crate, and we name no brands. What follows is how to measure, what the numbers mean for miniature and standard dachshunds, and how to handle a puppy who is still growing.

First impressions: why a dachshund breaks generic crate charts

A generic chart sorts dogs by weight. A dachshund’s weight says little about length. The breed standard published by the FCI, the international kennel federation, describes body length as being in a ratio of about 1.7–1.8 to 1 with height at the withers, so the dog is long by design. A crate that is tall enough but short on length forces your dachshund to curl or hunch, and one picked purely by weight can end up too big in the wrong direction.

The same standard sorts dachshunds into three sizes by chest circumference measured from at least 15 months old: standard males over 37 cm up to 47 cm and females over 35 cm up to 45 cm; miniature males over 32 cm up to 37 cm and females over 30 cm up to 35 cm; rabbit males 27 cm up to 32 cm and females 25 cm up to 30 cm (FCI Standard No. 148). Those are chest numbers, not crate numbers, but they show how much one breed spreads across sizes. Measure your own dog.

How to measure your dachshund for a crate

You need a soft tape and a few treats to keep your dog standing still. Our method follows the pet-store sizing guide and the vet-written crate guide, which agree on the steps.

  1. Length. With your dachshund on all four paws, measure from the tip of the nose to the base of the tail. Leave the tail out: Preventive Vet’s crate guide warns that including the full tail makes the crate too large.
  2. Height. Measure from the floor to the top of the head or the tip of the ears, whichever is taller. PetSmart’s sizing guide measures standing; Preventive Vet describes a sitting measurement. For a low dog the standing figure is the safer minimum if your dog likes to stand up and turn, so check the taller number.
  3. Add 2–4 inches to each. Both guides give that range as the minimum crate length and height.
  4. Skip width. Preventive Vet notes that crate width follows from the length and height, so you do not measure it.

Miniature vs standard: what the numbers look like

We cannot give you a single “dachshund crate size”, and any article that does is guessing. What we can do is show how the rule works. A dog whose nose-to-tail-base length measures 18 inches needs a crate about 20 to 22 inches long inside; a dog that measures 22 inches needs 24 to 26. Both land on the same band in the sizing chart, which lists dogs under 10 lb with crates 18–22 inches long and dogs of 11–25 lb with crates 24–30 inches long (PetSmart crate size chart). Use the weight band only to shortlist, then check the dog’s own tape numbers.

Growing puppies and divider panels

A puppy grows fast, so buy for the adult dog and make the space smaller for now. Both guides recommend a divider panel that “grows” the crate with the puppy, which saves buying several sizes. The reason for the small space is housetraining: if the puppy has too much room, they may toilet in one corner and sleep in the other (Preventive Vet). The same page suggests a large cardboard box as a do-it-yourself divider if the crate has no panel.

Set the divider so the puppy’s space equals their current nose-to-tail-base length plus 2–4 inches. The calculator above gives the number. Move the panel back every few weeks and re-measure; do not move it by feel. If your puppy cries or refuses to settle at night, that is a training problem more often than a size problem, and our puppy crate training guide and the bedtime crate and separation anxiety guide cover it.

Too big, too small: how to tell

Too small

Your dachshund cannot stand without hunching, turn around easily, or stretch out when lying down (PetSmart). A cramped crate adds stress, which is the opposite of what a den-like space is for.

Too big

The tell is a toilet corner. Dogs avoid soiling where they sleep, and a big crate lets them separate the two zones. Fix it with a divider before you blame the puppy.

Just right: the three-move test

Your dog should stand, turn a full circle, and lie flat on their side without touching both ends. If they can, and there is no room for a separate toilet corner, the crate is right.

Daily use: where to put it and how long the crate stays

Put the crate where the family is, with a firm mat rather than deep, slippery bedding. A crate is a short-term resting spot, not a place to park a dog all day. Dachshunds also have energetic bodies and active minds, so plan walks and play around crate time; see our dachshund energy level guide for realistic exercise amounts. If you are new to the breed, the first-time owner guide puts crate time into a daily routine.

Best for, skip if, and alternatives

Best for: owners of a miniature, standard or rabbit dachshund who want one crate that lasts, and puppy owners who need housetraining space control.

Skip the crate-size debate if your dog is already comfortable in a crate you own and passes the three-move test. Do not replace a crate that works.

Alternatives: a play pen with a small bed area, or a crate plus ramp for the car or bed. A dachshund’s back needs care in and out of a crate too: avoid high jumps, and read our stairs guide for how to keep the spine safe around everyday heights.

Final verdict

Rating the method, not a product: 9/10. Measure nose to tail base, add 2–4 inches, check the taller of head or ears for height, and use a divider for a puppy. The only gap is that sources disagree slightly about standing versus sitting height, so take the larger number. Bottom line: the correct dachshund crate is the smallest one in which your dog can stand, turn and stretch out.

Dachshund crate size FAQ

What size crate does a miniature dachshund need?
Measure from the nose to the base of the tail and add 2–4 inches for length. Do the same for height from the floor to the top of the head or ears. Weight charts place small dogs under 10 lb in crates about 18–22 inches long and 11–25 lb dogs in crates about 24–30 inches long, but your tape numbers decide.
What size crate does a standard dachshund need?
The same method applies. Standard dachshunds are bigger than miniatures, so the result is usually in the longer crate band, but always check the inside length in the listing and the manufacturer’s weight limit.
Should I include the tail when measuring?
No. Measure to the base of the tail. Including the whole tail gives a crate that is too large.
How big should a dachshund puppy’s crate be?
Buy a crate for the adult size and use a divider panel so the puppy’s space is their current length plus 2–4 inches. Move the divider back as they grow.
Why does a bigger crate make housetraining harder?
A puppy with extra room can toilet at one end and sleep at the other. A divider removes the spare corner.
Is a crate too small if my dachshund curls up?
Curling up to sleep is normal. The test is whether your dog can stand without hunching, turn around easily, and stretch out when lying down.
