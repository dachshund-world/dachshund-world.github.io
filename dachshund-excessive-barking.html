Dachshund Excessive Barking: Causes and Training Solutions

One trigger, one easier setup, one useful alternative: a practical way to approach a noisy day.

Reward-based practiceInteractive starting-task plannerEditorial draft · October 14, 2026

Living with dachshund excessive barking can make an ordinary delivery or evening meal feel overwhelming. You do not need to solve every noisy moment today: start with one predictable situation, make it easier, and teach your dog what to do instead.

Quick answer

Identify what happens immediately before the barking, reduce that trigger, and reward a useful alternative such as turning toward you or resting on a mat. Window alerts, requests for attention, excited greetings, and difficulty being alone need different plans. Aim for easier recovery and fewer repeated episodes, rather than a completely silent dog.

Choose your first barking-training task

This planner organizes an observation into a practical starting task. It does not identify your dog's emotions or promise a result. Select the situation you actually see, then change the difficulty if your dog cannot comfortably participate.



Why does my dachshund bark so much?

The situation matters more than the volume. Look at what your dog notices, what they do next, and what changes after the bark; the sound alone does not establish a cause.

Dogs Trust's behavior guidance describes barking as communication and distinguishes alerting, attention-seeking, excitement, and difficulty being left. It recommends recording triggers, changing the environment, and teaching alternative behaviors. Its advice also cautions that simply waiting for silence does not resolve how a dog feels about being alone.

Observe before naming the problem

Write “looked through the front window, barked, then followed me into the kitchen” instead of “being stubborn.” The first description tells you where to intervene. The second gives you no training task. Similarly, a bark during meal preparation and a bark at an approaching dog should occupy separate entries in your notes.

For an owner still finding a routine, the dachshund first-time owner guide provides broader context. For this project, keep the goal narrow: one trigger, one replacement behavior, and one consistent response from the household.

How can I reduce window and door barking?

Start by changing access to the view or arrival routine, then practice away from a real visitor. You want a rehearsal your dachshund can complete without being overwhelmed by the event itself.

Make the window task smaller

Choose a resting place that does not face the busiest window. Close the covering during predictable foot traffic, and have rewards ready before you open it for a practice session. Do not spend the whole session asking your dog to watch passing people. Your task is to notice a manageable distraction, invite a turn toward you, and move to the resting place.

If the dog immediately returns to the glass and resumes barking, close the view again. Record that the setup was too difficult, rather than repeating your cue more loudly. Next time, practice farther into the room or at a quieter time. A successful session can be small enough that nobody walking outside notices it happened.

Separate the knock from the visitor

Ask a household member to make a gentle practice sound while you remain with the dog. Keep the person outside the greeting sequence: no opening the door, excited conversation, or reaching toward the dog during the first rehearsal. If the sound already produces sustained barking, return to practicing the mat routine without it.

For real deliveries, prepare the resting area beforehand and ask people to text rather than knock when practical. This is management while a skill develops. You are allowed to make daily life easier; every parcel does not have to become a training test.

How do I teach a quiet alternative?

Teach a behavior you can recognize and reward: a head turn, following you, or remaining on a mat. Start in a quiet room, then introduce an easier version of the distracting situation.

In its 2025 clarification on humane training, the American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior reaffirmed its recommendation to use reward-based methods for training and behavior modification. That supports a plan centered on teaching and rewarding a response you want, rather than escalating corrections when barking continues.

Build a turn-and-follow response

  1. Choose a short cue, such as “this way.” Say it once in a quiet room and reward your dog for turning toward you.
  2. Repeat in another quiet location. Keep your body position easy to follow rather than leaning over your dog.
  3. Take a step toward the resting spot and reward following. Add another step only when the first is easy.
  4. Practice with a mild distraction. If your dog cannot turn, remove the distraction and return to the easier version.
  5. Use the cue during a manageable real event. Reward the turn and movement away, then allow a calm break.

These are suggested practice steps, not a standardized protocol or a promised timetable. The important observation is whether your dog understands the task in that setting. Knowing a cue in the kitchen does not tell you whether the same task is manageable beside an exciting doorway.

Make the mat a separate skill

Place a mat in the quiet practice area. Reward approaching it, standing on it, and remaining there comfortably. Keep your movements unhurried. If your dog gets up whenever you reach for a reward, make your delivery less distracting and reduce what you ask for on the next repetition.

What should I do about attention and excitement barking?

Plan rewards for quiet participation before the noisy routine starts. Check that your dog has an appropriate activity and an opportunity to go outside, rather than assuming every request should be ignored.

Prepare for dinner before preparing dinner

Set up the mat and reward a quiet moment before you start cooking. Begin with an easy rehearsal, such as moving one plate, rather than expecting your dog to wait through the entire evening meal. If barking starts, pause and make the next rehearsal easier. Record what happened without turning it into a debate with your dog.

Keep a household rule that everyone can follow: invite the dog to the same waiting place, reward the same quiet behavior, and avoid making food appear in direct response to repeated barking. The goal is a predictable route to attention and participation. It should be clear what works without your dog having to escalate.

Give excitement a calmer sequence

For a greeting rehearsal, have a familiar person enter quietly while you reward your dog in the resting area. Keep the first greeting brief. If the arrival becomes too much, the visitor can step back and you can return to the easier practice task. Avoid using a busy gathering as the first test.

Build quiet activities into the day without treating exercise as a guaranteed barking solution. Our dachshund exercise guide can help you think about the daily routine, while dachshund enrichment ideas offer options for searching and play. Choose activities your individual dog enjoys and can disengage from comfortably.

What if barking happens on walks or when I leave?

On walks, prioritize a setup with room to move away. When barking happens during your absence, collect observations and seek individual support instead of applying an attention-barking rule.

Make outdoor practice easy to leave

Choose a quiet route with visible side paths or open space. Rehearse your turn-and-follow cue before encountering another dog. When a dog appears, use the space available rather than walking directly toward it to test your progress. If turning is difficult, leave the situation calmly and select an easier route next time.

Write down the location and what made that encounter difficult: a narrow sidewalk, a dog coming around a corner, or several distractions together. You do not need to estimate an exact distance to learn something useful. A description such as “could turn away across the park, could not turn at the gate” helps plan the next outing.

Keep absence barking in its own plan

A recording can show whether barking begins with a departure, a hallway noise, or another event. Do not assume the quiet interval you hear from outside describes the entire absence. Arrange help with company where possible while a reward-based professional develops a plan with you.

For household context, see our guide to dachshund settling and separation routines and puppy crate-settling guide. Neither a closed crate nor a command to be quiet is a substitute for understanding what happens when you leave.

A step-by-step checklist for your first week

Use this week as an observation and setup project, not a deadline for stopping barking. The schedule below is our editorial worksheet; move more slowly whenever a task is difficult.

  1. Choose one situation. Pick something predictable, such as the dinner routine. Leave the other triggers on a separate list.
  2. Record a baseline. Note the trigger, whether your dog could turn toward you, and how the episode ended. Avoid provoking barking just to collect a number.
  3. Change one part of the environment. Cover a view, move the mat, or arrange quieter arrivals. Keep the other parts steady so the change is understandable.
  4. Practice without the trigger. Teach the alternative in a quiet place. Finish while participation is easy instead of waiting for your dog to disengage.
  5. Add an easy rehearsal. Use a small version of the event, such as handling one kitchen item or making a gentle practice knock.
  6. Review the notes together. Ask whether everyone used the same cue and reward rule. Change the setup before blaming inconsistent progress on the dog.
  7. Choose the next small step. Repeat an easy session or add one modest difficulty. If the plan is unclear or worsening, request individual training support.

Measure recovery as well as noise

Useful entries include “turned on the first cue,” “needed the window covered,” and “settled after following me.” If you count episodes, define an episode consistently in your own log. Do not compare a quiet weekday morning with a crowded weekend afternoon as though the conditions were identical.

Keep durations and counts as observations about your dog, not breed statistics. There is no evidence presented here for a dachshund-specific success percentage or a fixed number of days to resolve excessive barking. Improvement can mean an easier task completed more reliably before it means a quieter whole day.

Common mistakes that make the plan harder

The most useful correction is often to the setup. When the same rehearsal keeps failing, change what you are asking instead of adding more repetitions at the same difficulty.

  • Using one rule for every bark. Keep window events, greetings, attention requests, and absence observations in separate parts of your log.
  • Repeating an unlearned cue. Practice the response quietly first. A phrase your dog has not learned is not a reliable instruction.
  • Waiting for barking before noticing your dog. Arrange rewards for quiet participation earlier in the routine.
  • Making several changes at once. Change the view or practice location first; record the result before adding another variable.
  • Turning every arrival into a test. Use manageable rehearsals and a practical plan for real visitors.
  • Expecting a silent breed transformation. Choose observable goals such as turning away and settling, then review them honestly.

Dachshund excessive barking FAQ

Should I ignore every bark?
No. First identify the situation and check your dog's needs. A plan for attention requests is different from a plan for window alerts or difficulty being alone.
Can I teach a quiet cue?
You can build a cue around an observable quiet response, but teach it in an easy setting first. Turning toward you or going to a mat gives you a clear behavior to reward.
How long will barking training take?
This guide does not promise a fixed timeline. Track the same situation over repeated opportunities and look for easier redirection and recovery before increasing difficulty.
Will more walks stop the barking?
Do not treat extra walking as a guaranteed solution. Review the daily routine, but also identify the trigger and practice an alternative response that fits that situation.
What if my dachshund ignores me outside?
Choose a quieter place or more space from the distraction, and rehearse turning toward you there. If your dog cannot participate, leave the difficult setup and make the next practice easier.
What should I show a trainer?
Bring your trigger log, the household's current responses, and recordings of naturally occurring episodes. Ask for an individual reward-based plan and a clear explanation of how to adjust the difficulty.

Your next calm step

Dachshund excessive barking becomes a more manageable project when you stop treating every episode as the same event. Pick one trigger, create an easier setup, reward a clear alternative, and keep a simple log. You can make useful progress without testing your dog at every opportunity or promising complete silence.
