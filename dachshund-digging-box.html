Why Dachshunds Dig, and How to Redirect It With a Digging Box

No magic fixes, no punishment: an honest plan that gives a born digger one place to dig.

Digging-pattern decoderDIY digging boxReward-based

Dachshund digging is not a training failure — it's the breed doing exactly what it was built to do, just in your flower bed, under your fence, or in the middle of your duvet. Here's the honest truth up front: you probably won't stop a dachshund from digging, and you don't need to. What you can do is decide where it happens, and a well-set-up digging box is the most reliable tool we know for that.

Quick answer

Dachshunds dig because they were bred to hunt burrowing animals, and digging also relieves boredom, cools them down, and makes a cozy bed. Instead of fighting the instinct, give it a legal outlet: a sand or soil digging box (outdoors) or a blanket-and-paper dig box (indoors), seeded with buried toys and treats. Make it more rewarding than anywhere else, supervise the old spots for a few weeks, calmly redirect, and never punish after the fact.

Why do dachshunds dig so much?

Because digging was their job. Dachshunds were bred to follow badgers and other burrowing animals underground, so the urge to sniff, scrape, and tunnel is wired in rather than learned.

The Wikipedia entry on the breed sums up their origins: dachshunds were bred to scent, chase, and flush out badgers and other burrow-dwelling animals, while miniatures were bred to hunt smaller animals such as rabbits. That heritage shows up in the body too — strong front legs and big paddle-shaped paws make them very efficient diggers for their size.

We'll be straight with you: no amount of "no!" removes a breed instinct. Owners who try to ban digging entirely tend to end up with a frustrated dog who digs anyway, just more secretly. Owners who give the instinct a job usually end up with a dog who digs in one place and leaves the rest alone.

It's not only instinct

Instinct explains the urge; the situation explains when it boils over. Animal-welfare groups such as the Inland Valley Humane Society and SPCA list boredom and loneliness, being left alone for long periods, a barren area without toys, young dogs with energy and no outlet, chasing burrowing animals, cooling off in hot weather, and attention-seeking among the common reasons dogs dig. A dachshund often ticks several of these boxes at once.

How do you build a digging box for a dachshund?

A digging box is a defined spot filled with loose, safe material where your dog is allowed — and actively encouraged — to dig. Outdoors that means sand or soil; indoors it means blankets, paper, or a ball pit.

Outdoor digging box: step by step

  1. Pick the spot your dog already likes. If they always dig by the back fence, put the box nearby. Shade helps in summer.
  2. Frame it. A child's sandbox, a sturdy plastic tub, or a simple timber frame works. As a rough guide, we suggest about 24 × 36 inches for a miniature and 36 × 48 inches for a standard, with 8–12 inches of fill — enough to dig properly without the box taking over the yard.
  3. Fill with something soft and clean. Washed play sand, loose topsoil, or a mix. Avoid anything that stays soggy, and keep fertilized garden soil out of the box.
  4. Cover it when not in use if neighborhood cats might treat it as a litter box.
  5. Seed it. Bury a few favorite toys and some long-lasting chews, partly visible at first so the first dig pays off within seconds.

Indoor or balcony dig box

  1. Use a shallow cardboard box, a laundry basket, or a small paddling pool.
  2. Fill it with crumpled paper, old towels, fleece strips, or a few plastic ball-pit balls.
  3. Hide treats and a toy inside and let your dog root them out. Remove paper if your dog starts eating it rather than digging through it.

Make digging "pay": the box only works if it's more rewarding than your flower bed. Rotate what's buried, add a new toy now and then, and join in at first by scratching at the surface yourself. For more ideas to keep the box exciting, see our guide to dachshund toys and enrichment.

How do you teach a dachshund to dig in the box instead of the garden?

Make the box the best place to dig, make the old spots less available, and redirect calmly every time. Consistency over two to four weeks matters far more than any single trick.

Week 1: make the box exciting

  • Take your dog to the box several times a day and bury a treat while they watch. Say a cue like "dig it!" as they start.
  • Praise and reward as soon as they dig in the box. Let them "win" quickly.
  • Go out together — digging alone in an unsupervised yard is when old habits happen.

Weeks 2–3: block the old spots and redirect

  • Temporarily fence or cover favorite holes and garden beds with chicken wire laid flat, rocks, or a raised planter edge.
  • If you catch your dog digging elsewhere, interrupt calmly, take them to the box, and reward digging there. The Inland Valley Humane Society recommends exactly this: interrupt and promptly lead the dog to the designated digging area.
  • Keep unsupervised yard time short until the habit shifts.

Week 4 and beyond: maintain it

  • Bury something new a few times a week so checking the box stays worthwhile.
  • Slowly remove the barriers one at a time and watch what happens.
  • Expect the odd slip on exciting days — that's normal, not failure.

Can earthdog and nose work replace the urge to dig?

They can take a big edge off it. Giving a dachshund a structured way to hunt, sniff, and "go to ground" satisfies the same instinct that sends them into your borders.

Earthdog: the sport built for diggers

The AKC's Earthdog introduction explains that the sport is open to dachshunds and small terriers from six months of age. Dogs follow a scent through human-built tunnels to reach caged quarry — tame rats kept safely behind bars, so no animal is harmed. It's the closest thing to the job dachshunds were bred for, and many owners find it a fun weekend outlet.

Nose work at home

You don't need a club to use your dog's nose. In a study summarized by Purdue University's Center for Animal Welfare Science, pet dogs that did daily nosework for two weeks responded to an ambiguous cue more optimistically afterward than dogs that practiced heelwork. To be clear, that study didn't measure digging — but it supports something many owners notice: a dog who gets to use their nose every day tends to be more settled.

Simple ways to start: scatter part of a meal in the grass, hide treats under upturned cups, or play "find it" around one room. What about digging under the fence?

Fence digging deserves extra attention because a dachshund that gets out is at risk. Secure the boundary first, then work on why your dog wants to dig there.

  • Check the base of the fence for gaps and soft soil. Burying wire mesh along the bottom or laying it flat outward along the ground (an "L-footer") makes tunneling much harder.
  • Look for the attraction. Neighbor dogs, wildlife under a shed, or a busy footpath can all trigger boundary digging.
  • Supervise outdoor time until the fence is secure and the digging box has become the go-to spot.
  • Think about loneliness. Dogs left outside for long stretches dig more. If your dog digs mainly when you leave, our guide to dachshund separation anxiety and crate routines covers gentle alone-time training.

Common mistakes we see (and honestly made ourselves)

  • Punishing a hole you found later. Your dog can't link a scolding to something they did an hour ago. The Inland Valley Humane Society is blunt about this: never punish after the fact, because it won't stop the behavior and can make your dog fearful and anxious.
  • Building the box and walking away. An empty box of sand is boring. Seed it, visit it, and make it pay.
  • Leaving the old spots open. Every successful dig in the flower bed is practice. Block them while the new habit forms.
  • Filling holes with your dog watching. To a dachshund, you've just "dug" there too — and freshly turned soil is even more interesting.
  • Too much unsupervised yard time. A bored dachshund alone outside will find a job.
  • Expecting zero digging. A realistic goal is digging in the right place, not no digging at all.
  • Ignoring the weather. If your dog digs cool pits in summer, the fix is shade and water, not training.
  • Using deterrents that could hurt. Skip anything sharp, toxic, or designed to startle. Simple barriers and a better alternative work without risk.

Dachshund digging FAQ

Can you stop a dachshund from digging completely?
Honestly, rarely — and trying usually backfires. Digging is a core breed instinct. The realistic goal is to give it a dedicated outlet, like a digging box, and make the rest of the yard less available and less rewarding.
What should I put in a dog digging box?
Outdoors, washed play sand, loose topsoil, or a mix work well. Indoors, use crumpled paper, old towels, fleece strips, or ball-pit balls. Bury toys and chews in it so digging pays off quickly.
How long does it take for a dachshund to use a digging box?
Many dogs dig in a well-seeded box within the first few sessions. Moving the habit away from old spots usually takes a few weeks of supervision, blocking, and calm redirection.
Why does my dachshund dig in blankets and on the bed?
It's a normal nesting and burrowing behavior — many dachshunds scratch and circle before lying down. Give them a burrow bed or a blanket they're allowed to dig in, and it usually isn't a problem.
Should I fill in the holes my dachshund digs?
Yes, but do it when your dog isn't watching, and cover the spot with a barrier for a while. Fresh soil is extra tempting, so a filled hole without a barrier often gets dug again.
Is earthdog a good activity for dachshunds?
For many dachshunds, yes. Earthdog lets them follow scent through tunnels to caged quarry in a safe, structured way. According to the AKC, dachshunds can take part from six months of age.

The honest bottom line

Dachshund digging comes from generations of breeding, so the kindest and most effective plan is to stop fighting it and start directing it. Build a digging box, make it the best spot in the yard, block the old favorites for a few weeks, and add nose work or earthdog for the hunter inside. You'll still have a digger — just one who digs where you've said yes.
