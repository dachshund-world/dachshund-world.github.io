How to Hand Strip a Wirehaired Dachshund at Home, the Right Way

Keep the harsh, colorful wire coat your dachshund was bred to carry — with small, honest steps and a dog who stays comfortable throughout.

Quick answer

To hand strip a wirehaired dachshund at home, work on a dry, unbathed coat, hold a small section of skin taut, and pull only the loose, longest dead hairs in the direction of growth with your thumb and finger or a dull stripping knife. Go section by section — back, neck, sides, tail, then head — and stop the moment your dog is uncomfortable. Short, regular sessions ("rolling") keep the coat harsh and spare you a big strip later.

Learning how to hand strip a wirehaired dachshund at home is one of the kindest things you can do for that bristly coat — and for your dog's trust in you. Clippers are faster, but they trade the coat's texture and color for convenience. This guide shows you the honest version: what to pull, what to leave, and when to put the tools down.

What is hand stripping, and why does it matter for wirehaired dachshunds?

Hand stripping is pulling out dead outer hairs instead of cutting them, so that new harsh hair can grow in. It's the grooming method that matches the coat the breed is meant to have.

The Wikipedia article on dog grooming describes stripping as pulling the dead hair out of the coat of a non-shedding dog, using a stripping knife, a stripping stone, or the fingers, and notes that when it is done correctly the procedure is painless. A wire coat grows, then dies at full length, but the dead hair doesn't fall out on its own the way a smooth dachshund's coat does. If nobody removes it, it sits there, fades, and blocks the new coat coming through.

What the breed standard asks for

The Dachshund Club of America breed standard describes a uniform tight, short, thick, rough, hard outer coat with a finer, softer undercoat between the coarser hairs. The beard and eyebrows are the signature furnishings, the hair on the ears is almost smooth, and soft hair in the outer coat — especially on top of the head — is listed as a fault, as is long, curly, or wavy hair sticking out irregularly. You don't need a show dog to care about this: that harsh jacket is what makes the coat shed dirt and water the way it was designed to.

The ethical case for doing it right

Choosing to strip is choosing your dog's coat over your own convenience. It takes longer, and it asks you to read your dog closely and stop when they've had enough. That's the right trade. A good strip should never be a wrestling match — if it becomes one, the method needs to change, not your dog.

Hand stripping vs clipping vs carding: what's the difference?

Stripping pulls dead topcoat, carding pulls loose undercoat, and clipping cuts everything at a set length. Only stripping and carding work with the natural coat cycle.

Why clipping changes the coat

Writing for the grooming trade magazine Groomer to Groomer, Jennifer Bishop Jenkins explains that clipping a wire coat first leaves the paler, thinner, softer mostly-undercoat hairs, and that over time the follicles produce woolly hairs lacking pigment and texture. That's the "my wire turned into a fuzzy, faded teddy bear" story many owners recognize. She also notes that if a coat has already been clipped, it can be grown out using a guard comb rather than a bare blade until there's enough length to card and strip again.

Which tools do you actually need?

Your fingers, a fine-toothed metal comb, and one dull stripping knife are enough to start. Everything else is a nice-to-have.

  • Your thumb and forefinger — the gentlest tool there is. A rubber finger cot or a little grooming chalk improves grip.
  • A dull or medium stripping knife — it grips hair against your thumb; it should never cut. If you see blunt, cut ends instead of tapered roots, the knife is too sharp or your angle is wrong.
  • A stripping stone or pumice block — useful for flattening flyaways and lifting dead hair on the body.
  • A fine/medium metal comb — for checking your work and, used flat, for light carding.
  • Blunt-tipped scissors — for tidying feet and between pads only.
  • A non-slip surface at a comfortable height — a rubber mat on a sturdy table or your lap.
  • High-value treats or a lick mat — to make the whole experience worth your dog's while.

Protect that long back: Support your dog's chest and hindquarters whenever you lift them on or off a grooming surface, and never leave them alone on a table. Our guide on dachshunds, stairs, and jumping explains why controlled lifting matters so much for this breed.

How do you hand strip a wirehaired dachshund step by step?

Strip a dry, unwashed coat in small sections, pulling only loose hairs in the direction they grow, and keep sessions short. Bathe afterward, not before — clean hair is slippery and harder to grip.

If you'd like to watch the technique before you try it, this video from Polarose Dachshunds, a wirehaired dachshund breeder, shows a pet wire being stripped section by section, including the head, neck, legs, and beard, plus aftercare advice.

Before you start

  1. Pick a calm moment. After a walk and a toilet break, when your dog is ready to rest.
  2. Do the pinch test. Pull a few of the longest hairs on the back. If they slide out easily, the coat is ready. If they hold firm, wait and test again in two to three weeks.
  3. Comb through first. Remove tangles in the beard and leg hair so you can see the coat clearly.

The body (start here)

  1. Begin at the shoulders and back. This is the least sensitive area and where the coat is usually most ready.
  2. Stretch the skin. With your free hand, gently pull the skin taut behind the area you're working on. Taut skin is the single biggest comfort factor.
  3. Grip a few hairs and pull in the direction of growth. A short, quick, flicking motion — wrist steady, not a yank. Take small amounts; ten pinches of a few hairs beat one big handful.
  4. Work in rows. Move from the neck back toward the tail, then down the sides. Comb as you go to check for even length.
  5. Use the knife flat against your thumb, not like a blade. Hair is trapped and pulled, never sliced.

Neck, chest, and tail

  1. Neck and throat are kept short and tidy, blending smoothly into the shoulders. Go slowly — the skin here is thinner.
  2. Chest and underside need a lighter touch. Use your fingers rather than a knife on the belly, and skip the genital area entirely.
  3. Tail: strip so it tapers to a point. The breed standard asks for a thickly haired tail that tapers, not a flag.

Head, ears, eyebrows, and beard

  1. Top of the head: strip short and flat — this is where soft hair is most noticeable.
  2. Ears: the coat should be almost smooth. Pluck stray long hairs from the outside of the ear leather with your fingers, holding the ear flat on your palm.
  3. Eyebrows: leave them. Tidy only stray hairs so your dog can see clearly; the eyebrows are part of the look.
  4. Beard: comb it out and pluck only obviously long, wispy hairs. Don't strip it short.
  5. Feet: trim around the pads with blunt scissors for neat, round feet.

Finishing up

  1. Bathe if needed, then dry. Freshly stripped areas can look a little patchy for a week or two while new coat comes through.
  2. End on a good note. A short game or a chew afterward teaches your dog that grooming leads to good things — our dachshund toys and enrichment guide has calm ideas that work well here.

How do pin-wire, classic, and soft coats differ?

Wirehaired dachshunds don't all carry the same coat. The amount of work, and what you're aiming for, depends on which one your dog has.

Pin-wire coats

A very short, tight, harsh coat that can look almost smooth from a distance. These dogs need the least stripping — a light roll every so often, and a little tidying of the eyebrows and beard. Over-stripping a pin-wire can leave bare patches, so take less than you think.

Classic wire coats

The textbook harsh jacket with a good beard and eyebrows. These coats respond best to regular rolling: a few minutes on one or two areas every couple of weeks keeps the coat in layers, so there's always fresh hair coming through and your dog never has a "plucked" look.

Soft or "fuzzy" coats

These have more undercoat and soft outer hair, often with long furnishings. They can be the hardest to keep tidy. Carding the undercoat and stripping the longest hairs helps the coat lie flatter, but be honest with yourself: a soft coat won't turn into a show coat, and that's fine. The goal is a comfortable, tidy dog, not a different dog.

How often should you strip your wirehaired dachshund?

There's no universal schedule — let the coat tell you. Do the pinch test every few weeks and work on whichever areas are ready.

Many pet owners settle into one of two rhythms. The first is a full strip whenever the whole coat "blows" and lifts, followed by several weeks of growth. The second, which we recommend for most pets, is rolling: short sessions of 10–20 minutes every two to four weeks, working only on the areas that are ready. Rolling is gentler, easier to fit into a week, and avoids the bare, scruffy phase in between. These are our editorial suggestions, not a rule — your dog's coat and comfort set the pace.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Stripping a freshly washed coat. Clean hair is slippery. Strip first, bathe after.
  • Pulling hair that isn't ready. If it holds firm, it's still alive. Forcing it is what makes stripping uncomfortable.
  • Loose skin. Always hold the skin taut with your other hand.
  • Using a sharp knife or sawing motion. Cut ends mean you're cutting, not stripping — the coat will soften just as if you'd clipped it.
  • Doing the whole dog in one marathon. Two short sessions beat one long ordeal.
  • Stripping the eyebrows and beard short. Tidy them; don't remove them.
  • Restraining a dog who's clearly saying no. Lip licking, turning away, freezing, or growling mean stop. Pushing through teaches your dog that handling is scary — read our guide on why dachshunds bite and how to prevent it if handling is already tense.
  • Clipping "just once" to save time. One clip can set a wire coat back by months.
  • Unsupported lifting. Always support the chest and rear when putting your dog on or off a surface.

When is a professional groomer the better choice?

Choose a groomer who hand strips when your dog's coat has been clipped for years, when your dog finds handling very stressful, or when you want a show-standard finish. There's no shame in it — integrity means doing what's best for your dog, not proving you can do it all yourself.

A good compromise many owners like: book one professional strip, watch or ask questions, then maintain the coat at home by rolling. Ask the groomer to confirm they hand strip rather than clip, and to show you which areas grow fastest on your dog.

Hand stripping FAQ

Does hand stripping hurt a wirehaired dachshund?
When the coat is ready and the skin is held taut, the dead hair comes out with little resistance and it shouldn't hurt. If hairs hold firm or your dog flinches, the coat isn't ready in that area — stop and try again in a couple of weeks.
Can I strip my dachshund with just my fingers?
Yes. Fingers are the gentlest tool and ideal for beginners, ears, and the underside. A rubber finger cot or a little grooming chalk improves grip. A dull stripping knife simply speeds things up on the body.
Can a clipped wire coat be restored?
Often it can be improved. Let the coat grow out (a guard comb rather than a bare blade if you must tidy it), then card the soft undercoat and strip the longest hairs as they become ready. It can take several coat cycles, and some coats never fully regain their original harshness.
Should I bathe my dachshund before stripping?
No. Strip a dry, unwashed coat because clean hair is slippery and harder to grip. Bathe afterward if your dog needs it.
At what age can I start stripping a wirehaired puppy?
Start getting your puppy used to gentle handling, combing, and the feel of a few hairs being plucked as early as you like, in very short, treat-filled sessions. Real stripping begins once the puppy coat starts to lift and the pinch test shows hairs coming out easily.
How long does a home strip take?
A full strip can take a few hours for a beginner, so split it across several days. Rolling sessions of 10–20 minutes on one or two areas are much easier on both of you.

Do right by the wire coat

Hand stripping a wirehaired dachshund at home is slower than clipping, but it keeps the coat your dog was meant to have — harsh, colored, and tidy — and it builds trust every time you stop before your dog has to tell you to. Test the coat, take small amounts, hold the skin taut, and let the coat set the schedule. That's grooming you can feel good about.
