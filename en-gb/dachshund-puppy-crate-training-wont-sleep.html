Dachshund Puppy Won't Sleep in the Crate? A UK Night Plan

A dachshund puppy that screams the moment the crate door closes is not being stubborn. In a British house or flat, the fix is usually the same: build the crate as a normal daytime place, keep the first nights short and boring, and plan the toilet breaks instead of hoping.

Quick answer

Put the crate where the puppy can hear you, feed meals inside it with the door open, and close the door for seconds before minutes. Expect one toilet break at night for a young puppy, and do it on a lead with the lights low — no play, no chat.

Set the crate up for a UK home

Use a crate just big enough for the puppy to stand, turn and lie flat — a dachshund needs length more than height, so measure nose to tail rather than guessing from weight. Put it in a corner, out of the draught from the front door, and away from a radiator that clicks on and off all night.

A crate in a UK front room often sits on a wooden floor; put a non-slip mat underneath so the crate does not slide when the puppy shifts. Cover two or three sides with a breathable blanket, never the front, and leave the door open during the day.

The first three nights

Do not start with a closed door and a full night. Day one: feed every meal in the crate, door open. Day two: throw a treat in and close the door for five seconds, then twenty. By night three, close the door after the last toilet trip, sit nearby for a few minutes, and go to bed.

If the puppy wakes and settles within a minute or two, wait. If the crying escalates into real distress, take the puppy out for a quiet toilet break, then return it to the crate. The aim is that the crate predicts sleep, not a fight.

Toilet breaks at night

A young dachshund puppy cannot hold all night. A common rule of thumb is one hour for every month of age, plus one, so a three-month puppy may manage around four hours — and many still need a break. Carry the puppy outside, keep the trip dark, quiet and on a lead, and put it straight back.

Lift the water bowl an hour before the last trip, but do not withhold water on a warm evening. If the puppy is wet every night at the same time, set an alarm fifteen minutes earlier rather than waiting for the accident.

Crying, neighbours and flats

In a flat, a crying puppy is a neighbour problem as well as a training problem. Talk to the neighbours before the first night, keep the crate away from the shared wall, and add a soft background sound. Do not leave the puppy to cry for an hour to "learn"; that usually makes the next night worse.

If the puppy is fine during the day but panics at night, the crate is probably too far from you. Move it to the bedroom for a week, then shift it out in stages.

Buying a puppy in the UK: what to check first

In England and Wales, Lucy's Law means puppies should come directly from the breeder or a rescue, not through a third-party dealer. Ask to see the mother with the litter, check that the puppy is microchipped, and get the vaccination record before you bring it home. The Kennel Club's Assured Breeder Scheme is one place to start a search, not a guarantee on its own.

Questions UK owners ask

How long can a dachshund puppy hold its bladder at night?
A common rule of thumb is one hour for every month of age, plus one, and most puppies still need one night break at three to four months. Do not test the limit: a calm, quiet toilet trip on a lead is better than a wet crate.
Should the crate be in the bedroom for the first nights?
For many puppies yes. Being able to hear and smell you settles the first nights faster, and it makes short toilet trips easier. Move the crate gradually if you want the dog sleeping downstairs later.
Why does my puppy cry as soon as I close the crate?
Usually because the crate has not been built up as a good place while the door was open. Feed meals in it, throw treats in during the day, and close the door for seconds before minutes. If crying is paired with real distress, stop and go back a step.
