Best Toys for Dachshunds: Mental and Physical Stimulation That Protects Their Backs

Sniff, dig, search and tug: a friendly, back-aware guide to choosing and rotating toys your dachshund will actually love.

IVDD-aware playFree weekly toy plannerEditorial draft · October 11, 2026
Quick answer

The best toys for dachshunds are ones that let them do what they were bred for: sniff, dig, search, and "catch" something, all at ground level. Rotate snuffle mats and food puzzles for the brain, low tug and short rolling-ball games for the body, and a dig box for instinct. Skip anything small enough to swallow, anything that splinters, and games that make your dog leap or twist in the air.

Finding the best toys for dachshunds is less about the shiniest product and more about matching your dog's badger-hunting brain to their long, sensitive back. If your doxie shreds plush toys in ten minutes, buries chews in the sofa, or barks at nothing in the evening, you're not doing anything wrong. You just have a clever little hunter who's looking for a job, and the right toy rotation can give them one.

Why do dachshunds need mental stimulation, not just walks?

Because dachshunds were bred to think and work underground, a walk alone often tires the legs but not the brain. A bored dachshund usually invents its own job, and that job tends to be digging, chewing, or barking.

The name gives it away. The AKC's Earthdog guide explains that "dachshund" is German for "badger dog" and that the breed was developed as a multipurpose hunter that also worked underground. That heritage still shows up on your living room rug: the nose goes down, the front paws start scraping, and the stubborn streak kicks in.

The nose is the biggest muscle you can tire out

Sniffing is not just a cute habit. In a study summarized by Purdue University's Center for Animal Welfare Science, Duranton and Horowitz (2019) had one group of pet dogs do daily nosework for two weeks and a control group do heelwork. Only the nosework dogs approached an ambiguous bowl faster afterward, which the researchers read as a more optimistic outlook.

For you, that translates into something simple: a few minutes of sniff-and-search games each day is real enrichment, not a substitute for "proper" exercise. If you're still working out how much physical activity your dog needs, our guide to dachshund energy levels and daily exercise pairs nicely with the toy plan below.

Boredom often looks like a behavior problem

Many of the "naughty" behaviors owners write in about, like shredded cushions, holes in the garden, or nonstop alert barking, overlap with an under-employed hunting dog. Enrichment won't fix every case, but it's one of the first things worth trying. If the noise is the main issue, our article on why dachshunds bark so much walks through other causes to rule out.

What makes a toy safe for a dachshund?

A safe dachshund toy is too big to swallow, tough enough not to break into chunks for your dog's chewing style, and used in a way that keeps all four paws on the ground. When in doubt, supervise.

The ASPCA's position statement on dog chews and treats is refreshingly direct: excessively small products or products that fragment should not be offered to any dog, owners should pick a size consistent with their pet's size and chewing habits, and they should actively watch their dog while it chews. It also notes that small chews can be swallowed whole, causing choking or blockage, and that a dog swallowing a chunk of chew material is a reason to call your vet right away.

Size: think "bigger than it looks necessary"

Dachshunds have a surprisingly big mouth and a lot of jaw power for their height. A ball that fits fully behind the back teeth is too small. Choose toys sized for the next size up from "toy breed," especially for standards.

Hardness: the thumbnail check

A quick rule of thumb many owners use: if you can't press a slight dent into a chew with your thumbnail, it may be harder than your dog's teeth. Antlers, hard nylon bones, and cooked bones are the usual suspects. This is a practical home check, not a guarantee, so ask your vet at the next dental exam which chews suit your dog's mouth.

Movement: protect the back

Dachshunds are predisposed to intervertebral disc disease (IVDD). Cornell's Riney Canine Health Center lists the breed under predisposition and advises keeping an ideal body condition and providing steps or ramps to reduce jumping on and off objects. Apply the same thinking to play: low throws along the floor, gentle tug held at your dog's head height, and no "jump for it" games.

Stop play and call your vet if your dachshund yelps when picked up, hunches, becomes reluctant to move, wobbles, or drags a paw. Don't "test" the back with more play.

Which toy types are best for dachshunds?

The best mix covers four needs: sniffing, problem-solving, gentle physical play, and safe chewing. No single toy does all four, which is why rotation beats buying one "perfect" toy.

Snuffle mats and scatter feeding

For most dachshunds, this is the best value toy you can buy. Sprinkle part of a meal into the fabric and let your dog forage. It's quiet enough for apartments, gentle enough for seniors and dogs on crate rest (check with your vet first), and easy to scale by hiding food deeper.

Food puzzles

Start with easy level-one puzzles. A dachshund that can't win quickly may give up or decide to destroy the puzzle instead. Increase difficulty only after your dog solves the current one confidently a few days in a row.

Burrow and dig toys

Plush "hide-a-toy" logs or a cardboard box filled with crumpled paper and a few treats let your dog dig and pull out "prey" without destroying the garden. Remove any squeaker that starts poking out.

Tug toys

Tug is excellent physical play when you keep it gentle. Hold the toy low so your dog pulls backward with the front feet on the floor, let them win often, and teach a "drop it" for treats so the game stays polite.

How much toy time does a dachshund need each day?

Most adult dachshunds do well with two or three short enrichment sessions a day on top of their walks, roughly 5–15 minutes each. Puppies and seniors need shorter, more frequent sessions.

These numbers are our editorial starting points, not a veterinary prescription. Watch your own dog: a well-enriched dachshund settles and naps after a session; an over-stimulated one gets frantic, nippy, or keeps barking. Adjust from there. For puppies, short games also fit nicely around rest and crate time, which our guide on dachshund puppies that won't sleep in the crate covers in detail.

Remember the treats count

Food puzzles make it very easy to overfeed. Take puzzle food from the daily meal rather than adding extra treats, since extra weight puts more strain on the spine.

How do you set up a toy rotation step by step?

Rotation keeps toys "new" without buying more. Put most toys away and bring out only a few at a time.

  1. Audit what you have. Throw away anything cracked, frayed, small enough to swallow, or worn down. The ASPCA specifically advises discarding chews before they become excessively worn.
  2. Sort into four boxes: sniff (snuffle mat, scatter games), puzzle (food puzzles), physical (tug, balls), and chew.
  3. Leave out only two or three toys, one durable chew plus one or two others. Store the rest out of sight.
  4. Swap every two to three days. The returning toy often gets the same excitement as a new one.
  5. Schedule sniff games before alone time. A 10-minute snuffle session before you leave can help your dog settle.
  6. Play physical games on grippy surfaces. Rugs or grass, never polished floors where your dog skids and twists.
  7. Check toys after every session for loose parts, then put interactive ones away. Toys you can't supervise shouldn't stay out.

Free enrichment idea: Hide a few kibbles under three upturned paper cups and let your dog find the right one. Then try "find it" with treats hidden around one room. Nose games cost nothing and suit every dachshund, including the stair-shy ones; if your dog struggles with steps, see our guide on whether dachshunds can use stairs.

What are the most common dachshund toy mistakes?

The big three are toys that are too small, games that involve jumping, and leaving destructible toys out unsupervised.

  • Buying "toy breed" sizes. Dachshunds are small but strong chewers. Tiny balls and mini chews are swallowing hazards.
  • Throwing balls high or up the stairs. Catching in mid-air, skidding stops, and repeated stair sprints all stress the spine.
  • Frisbee and jump-for-the-toy games. These are great for some breeds and a poor fit for a long, low back.
  • Leaving plush toys out all day. Squeakers and stuffing are among the easiest things for a determined hunter to "kill" and swallow.
  • Using very hard chews. If it hurts when you tap it on your knee, it's probably too hard for teeth.
  • Too much difficulty too fast. A puzzle that's impossible becomes a chew toy. Keep it winnable.
  • Forgetting puzzle calories. Every treat in a dispenser counts toward the daily total.
  • Punishing digging instead of redirecting it. Give your dog a legal place to dig, like a dig box or a sandpit, and reward using it.

Can enrichment toys help with specific dachshund problems?

Enrichment can take the edge off boredom-related chewing, digging, and some attention barking, but it doesn't replace training, a vet check, or professional behavior help when a problem is serious.

Destructive chewing

Give a legitimate chewing outlet every day and swap it before it gets boring. Puppies chew more while teething, so puppy-appropriate rubber toys help.

Digging

Dachshunds dig because generations of breeding said "go get it." A dig box, a burrow toy, or a blanket pile with hidden treats lets them do it where you choose.

Restlessness on recovery or crate rest

If your dog is recovering from a back injury, very calm nose work like a snuffle mat may be one of the few games your vet allows. Always get explicit approval first and follow the activity limits you're given.

Dachshund toy FAQ

What is the single best toy for a dachshund?
For most dachshunds, a snuffle mat gives the best return: it uses the nose, slows eating, keeps all four paws on the floor, and works for puppies, adults, and seniors. Pair it with one durable chew and rotate the rest.
Are tennis balls safe for dachshunds?
A standard tennis ball can be fine for rolled, supervised play, but stop if your dog chews it apart, since fuzz and rubber pieces can be swallowed. Never use tiny balls that can lodge in the throat, and avoid high throws that make your dog jump.
Should dachshunds play tug of war?
Yes, gentle tug is a good game if you keep the toy low, let your dog pull backward with the front feet on the ground, avoid lifting or swinging them, and teach a reliable drop cue.
Why does my dachshund destroy every toy?
Ripping "prey" apart is close to what the breed was bred for. Offer tougher rubber toys, supervise plush toys, give a daily chewing and digging outlet, and remove toys once pieces start coming off.
Can a dachshund with IVDD still play with toys?
Often, yes, but only within the limits your veterinarian sets. Calm, floor-level nose games are usually the gentlest option; running, tug, and ball play should wait for explicit approval.
How many toys should a dachshund have?
There's no magic number. As a practical starting point, eight to ten toys in rotation, with two or three available at a time, keeps interest high without clutter.

The bottom line on dachshund toys

The best toys for dachshunds feed the hunter in them — sniffing, digging, searching, and a bit of polite tug — while keeping that long back on the ground. Choose big, sturdy toys, supervise chewing, rotate every few days, and let your dog win. A tired nose usually makes for a calmer, happier dachshund.
