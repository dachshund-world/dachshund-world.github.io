Dachshund Puppy Cries When You Leave: A Step-by-Step Alone-Time Training Plan Before Going Back to Work

If your dachshund puppy cries when you leave, the useful question is not how to make the noise stop today but how long your puppy can currently stay calm without you. That number may be ten seconds, and it is still a perfectly good place to start before your first day back at work.

Quick answer

Practise absences that are short enough for your puppy to stay relaxed, lengthen them a little after quiet trials, and shorten them after crying. Start inside the house, film what happens once you go out, keep exits and returns boring, and line up a person to cover any hours your puppy cannot yet manage.

Alone-time log: what should your next practice step be?

Run one short absence, record what happened, and let the log suggest the next one; the step sizes are our own cautious rule of thumb, not a tested standard.

Next-step ladder and return-to-work gap check

    The log lives only on this page and disappears when you reload it, so copy the lines you want to keep into your phone. Write down the time of day too: a puppy that is calm after a morning walk may struggle in a busy evening, and that is information, not failure.

    To see the stages before you try them, watch this Kikopup lesson, which moves from food to a bed or crate, then adds duration, movement and finally real time alone (the alone-time section starts around 9:25).

    Why does a dachshund puppy cry when you leave?

    Mostly because being alone is a skill your puppy has not learned yet. Blue Cross notes that dogs are very social animals and that being alone does not come naturally to most of them, and young dogs are also easily bored and full of energy.

    That does not mean every whine is the same. One puppy grumbles for a moment and goes to sleep. Another paces, scratches at the gate and howls without a pause. Your plan should respond to which one you actually have, and you cannot know that from the front step.

    Is it the dachshund in them?

    Many owners hear that dachshunds are simply a clingy, noisy breed and there is nothing to do. The better evidence does not support giving up. In the Generation Pup study of 145 puppies at six months of age, breed, sex and source showed no significant association with separation-related behaviour. That was a study of many kinds of puppy, not of dachshunds alone, so it cannot tell you how your puppy will behave. It does suggest that daily handling is a better place to look than the pedigree.

    How long can a dachshund puppy be left alone?

    Only as long as it can stay relaxed, and the RSPCA advises no more than four hours even for dogs that cope well. The same advice says the right length depends on the individual dog.

    The RSPCA’s guidance on training a dog to be left alone gives the reason for that ceiling: dogs need chances to exercise, go to the toilet and spend time with people. For a young puppy the practical limit is usually set by whichever runs out first, calm or bladder.

    Quiet is not automatically calm. The Royal Veterinary College’s puppy study counted lip-licking, looking at the door and yawning as passive signs of anxiety. Its lead author also described a puppy that paced and howled for hours while the owners believed it had slept. A phone, tablet or cheap camera pointed at the resting area settles the question. The RSPCA also recommends filming your dog from time to time to catch hidden distress.

    Set up the room before the first trial

    Choose one comfortable place and make it ordinary long before it means “goodbye”. Blue Cross advises against using the area only when you are about to leave, because the puppy can learn to connect it with being left.

    What goes in

    • A bed and fresh water.
    • A long-lasting chew or stuffed toy that you have already watched your puppy use safely, sized so it cannot be swallowed.
    • A toilet option if the stretch is longer than your puppy can hold.
    • Something that smells of you, if your puppy likes to rest on it rather than shred it.
    • Nothing it can climb, tip over or chew through, including cables and low furniture.

    Gate, crate or pen?

    A stair gate is a gentle first barrier because the puppy can still see, smell and hear you. A crate suits a puppy that already naps in it by choice; check our dachshund crate size guide before buying one, and do not use a crate for stretches the puppy cannot manage. Whatever you choose, our list of toys and enrichment ideas for dachshunds can help you find something worth staying with. A dog that enjoys digging may like a supervised digging box session earlier in the day, rather than discovering the carpet while you are out.

    Take the edge off first

    Offer a toilet break, some calm play or a short sniffing walk and, if it suits your routine, a small meal. The RSPCA suggests getting back from the walk about half an hour before you go, so the puppy has time to wind down. Aim for pleasantly tired, not wound up from a frantic game.

    Step-by-step alone-time training plan

    Move to the next stage only when the current one is boring for your puppy. Several tiny sessions spread across the day are easier to learn from than one long ordeal, and you can stop on a success.

    Stage 1: relaxed with you in the room

    Settle the puppy on the bed with its chew and sit nearby without chatting. Quietly reward calm. If even this is hard, stay here for a few days; nothing later will work if the bed itself is stressful.

    Stage 2: distance, while still visible

    Take a step or two away, come back before the puppy needs to follow, and carry on. The RSPCA stages go the same way: stay, move away and return, then gradually go further and for longer. If your puppy gets up and worries, go back to the previous distance.

    Stage 3: behind a gate

    Give the chew, close the gate and do ordinary things within sight and earshot. After a few minutes, or sooner for a nervous puppy, open it while your puppy is still relaxed. Let the puppy choose whether to stay or come out.

    Stage 4: out of sight, still at home

    Step around a corner for a few seconds and return. Then make it longer, with some very short repeats mixed in so each exit does not predict a harder one. Blue Cross suggests building up to about half an hour behind the gate while you are busy elsewhere in the house before you begin leaving home.

    Stage 5: through the front door

    Treat the front door as a new exercise and start small again. Step out, close it, come straight back. Then wait a few seconds, then a minute. Use the camera from outside and return while the puppy is still coping. Vary the length once you have reached several minutes comfortably, so your puppy is not counting up to a fixed breaking point.

    Stage 6: realistic departures

    Now rehearse the real thing: breakfast, walk, getting dressed, keys, door, and a drive around the block. Extend gradually toward a work-length block. The RSPCA says that once a dog is happy to be left for up to an hour, longer periods should not be a problem, but check this on your own camera before relying on it.

    What about the crying that has already started?

    If you misjudged a step and the puppy is distressed, return. Wait for a brief breath of quiet if one comes naturally, but do not stand outside while panic climbs. Come in without a big greeting, let things settle, and make the next trial easy enough to succeed. One poor trial is a data point. Ten of them in a row means the steps are too big.

    If the noise also happens when you are home, for example at the window or the doorbell, that is a different job; see our calm plan for dachshund barking.

    What if your puppy panics at the sight of your keys?

    Practise the cues without leaving, until they stop predicting anything. Pick up your keys, bag or coat while the puppy is relaxed, then put them down and make tea.

    Blue Cross describes this approach and says it may take several days before the puppy looks calm at those sights and sounds; only then restart short exits. Keep it light: a handful of repeats scattered through the day beats jingling keys for ten minutes.

    Leaving and coming home

    Say little and move normally. In the Royal Veterinary College trial, 34 new puppy owners were randomly given different guidance and filmed their puppies. The researchers found no significant effect on specific signs of separation distress overall. Puppies whose owners were advised to leave and return calmly, exercise them first and provide toys or treats did spend more time resting. Puppies in the gradual-practice and long-lasting-treat advice group rested more in a supervised test and showed fewer passive signs of stress. The group was small and the researchers call for larger studies, so take it as encouragement, not a guarantee.

    A calm return is not a cold one. Greet your puppy once the leaping has eased, take it out to toilet, and then enjoy each other.

    Can you finish the training before going back to work?

    Sometimes, but plan as though you might not. No published timetable can promise that a puppy will manage your shift by a particular Monday, so build cover into the first weeks and let the training catch up.

    This worry is very familiar. In a Dogz Online forum thread from 2008, an owner of a three-month-old miniature dachshund had two days left before work. Another member recommended a steady morning routine and calm greetings. The owner later reported that the first day went well after tiring the puppy out, leaving while it slept and returning for breaks from a workplace across the street. It is one household’s story, but notice how much of the success came from short stretches and check-ins.

    The table is a way to organise effort, not a deadline for your puppy. If you are still at stage 2 on day three, stay there and rely on cover. Options include working from home for part of the week, splitting leave between adults, a lunchtime visit, a sitter or a trusted friend who keeps the puppy at their home.     Checklist for the night before

    • Camera charged and aimed at the resting area, with the live view tested away from home Wi-Fi.
    • Helper’s visit time written down.
    • Chew prepared, water filled, toilet spot ready.
    • Walk time set so your puppy has time to settle before you go.
    • A plan for what you will do if the camera shows panic, including who can get there.

    Common mistakes that keep the crying going

    Making one long jump

    Going from two minutes to two hours because Monday has arrived asks for a skill the puppy does not have. Use cover for the hours and keep practising the minutes.

    “Let them cry it out”

    This is the most common myth. The training here is built on the opposite idea: the RSPCA says never to leave a dog so long that it becomes distressed.

    Telling the puppy off afterwards

    A puddle or chewed cushion found at five o’clock cannot be explained to a puppy by scolding. The RSPCA warns that punishment on your return makes separation-related behaviour worse, because the dog learns to worry about you coming home.

    Assuming a TV is company

    Background sound may mask outside noises, and Blue Cross suggests a quiet talk radio. Yet in the small RVC study, puppies showed more passive anxiety signs and less eating and playing when a TV or radio was left on. Test it on camera instead of assuming.

    Saving every treat for departures that are too hard

    A special chew cannot cover a step that is far beyond your puppy. If the chew is ignored, treat that as a signal to go back a stage.

    When to ask for help

    If your puppy cannot manage even seconds after careful practice, hurts itself trying to escape, or gets worse as you go, ask your vet for a referral to a qualified clinical animal behaviourist. Asking early is easier than undoing months of rehearsal.

    Dachshund puppy crying when you leave: FAQ

    Should I ignore my dachshund puppy crying when I leave?
    Do not use rising crying as a training method. If your puppy is distressed, the step was too long: come back without a fuss and make the next trial shorter. A brief grumble that fades on its own is different, and you can repeat that step.
    How long does alone-time training take?
    There is no fixed number of days. Blue Cross says building up in the house can take a few days for some puppies and longer for others, so plan around your log and arrange cover for any gap that is still open when work starts.
    How long can I leave a dachshund puppy alone?
    The RSPCA advises that dogs are left for no longer than four hours, while noting that it depends on the individual dog. Treat that as a ceiling, not a target, and expect a young puppy to need far shorter stretches.
    Is it better to leave the TV or radio on?
    The evidence is mixed. Blue Cross suggests a quiet radio, but puppies in a small Royal Veterinary College study showed more passive signs of anxiety and less eating and playing when a TV or radio was left on. Film your puppy both ways and keep what works.
    Are dachshunds more likely to cry when left than other breeds?
    The Generation Pup study of 145 six-month-old puppies found no significant association between breed and separation-related behaviour. It was not a dachshund study, so judge the puppy in front of you.
    Should I use a crate or a pen for alone time?
    Either can work if your puppy is already happy resting there. A stair gate, crate or pen should be introduced while you are at home, never only at the moment you walk out.

    Summary: build quiet time before you need it

    When a dachshund puppy cries when you leave, shrink the problem until the puppy can succeed, then grow it slowly. Film the absences, repeat the easy ones, back off after hard ones, keep your comings and goings dull, and book help for the hours you have not trained yet. Your next step is small: set up the bed and camera today and log one trial.
